# Every big city should copy New York's congestion charge

For twenty years American politicians said that charging drivers to enter a city centre could not be done here. It was too European, too unpopular and too easy to kill in court. New York has now done it for the best part of two years, and the results are in. Other cities should stop studying the idea and start copying it.

The scheme itself is simple. Since 5 January 2025, most drivers entering Manhattan's central business district pay a toll, which is collected automatically. A passenger car pays $9 during the day, and the charge drops to $2.25 overnight. Number plates are read at 210 detection points around the zone, which between them carry more than 1,400 scanners. There are no booths and no queues.

The traffic numbers moved at once. In March 2025 the Metropolitan Transportation Authority reported 23% fewer vehicles entering the zone than a year earlier. Over the first full year there were an estimated 17 million fewer vehicle entries. Buses ran faster, and one analysis found that subway ridership rose 7.7% from 2024 to 2025.

The money followed. The toll brought in $38.6 million in its first month, and in October 2025 the MTA sold $230 million of bonds to start paying for the first projects. That is new signals, new lifts and new trains, paid for by the people who was causing the delays.

The real price of every thing, what every thing really costs to the man who wants to acquire it, is the toil and trouble of acquiring it. A driver who crawled across Midtown used to pay that price in time, and so did everybody stuck behind him. Now he pays some of it in dollars, and the dollars go somewhere useful.

Opponents promised that the charge would wreck the city's economy. It did not. Shops inside the zone have seen their takings rise by 30% since the toll began. The theatres are busy, the pavements are fuller, and empty storefronts in the zone are filling up again.

They also promised that the courts would stop it. Sean Duffy, the federal transportation secretary, revoked the scheme's federal approval on 19 February 2025 and ordered the tolls to end. New York kept collecting them. On 13 March 2026 a federal judge ruled that the revocation was unlawful, and the legal argument were effectively over.

So here is my case. New York's traffic fell after the toll came in, so the same charge would work in every city in the world. The physics of a traffic jam do not change at the state line, and nor does the logic of a price. If drivers respond to nine dollars in Manhattan, they will respond to nine dollars in Houston, Los Angeles or Atlanta.

Mayors who still hesitate should ask themselves what they are waiting for. The technology exists, the law has been tested and the public has come round. Every year of delay is another year of buses stuck in traffic and budgets stuck in deficit, and their is no longer any excuse for it.
