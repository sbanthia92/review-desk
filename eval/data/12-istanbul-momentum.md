# Momentum is real, and Istanbul proves it

Analytics people love to tell us that momentum is a myth. They have spreadsheets which say a match is only a string of independent events, and that the feeling you get when a stadium turns is a story we tell ourselves afterwards. I was watching on the night of the 2005 Champions League final, and I do not need a spreadsheet to know what I saw.

That final was over by half-time. Paolo Maldini opened the scoring inside the first minute, and Andriy Shevchenko added two more before the break. Milan were strolling, Liverpool were a mess, and the only question left was how many it would be. I remember a friend putting his coat on and saying he would watch the second half out of duty.

Then something changed that no model can measure. Rafael Benitez sent on Dietmar Hamann at the interval, the Liverpool end started singing, and Steven Gerrard headed in a cross that should of been a consolation. Liverpool scored three times in sixteen minutes, and a final that had been dead was suddenly level at 3-3.

Nothing is so painful to the human mind as a great and sudden change. You could see it in the Milan players, who were among the most experienced footballers in Europe and suddenly could not complete a simple pass. Each of them were looking at the bench for help, and the bench had none to give.

Liverpool held on through extra time, thanks to a double save from Jerzy Dudek that I still cannot explain, and then won the shoot-out 3-2. Milan got their revenge against the same opponents two years later in Athens, but hardly anybody talks about that final, and that tells you which of the two nights mattered.

The numbers back up what our eyes tell us. Teams that score first after half-time go on to win 75% of the time. The affect on the opposition is obvious to anyone who has played the game at any level: heads drop, passes go sideways and the crowd does the rest. You cannot put that in a model, but you can feel it from the back row of the stand.

This is why I have no patience for managers who try to sit on a lead. Liverpool came back from three goals down, so no lead is ever safe and managers should never try to defend one. If a three-goal cushion in a European final is not enough, then nothing is, and the only sensible response is to keep attacking until the whistle goes.

The spreadsheet crowd will say that one night proves nothing. I say that one night is exactly what proves it, because every player on that pitch knew the game had turned before the second goal went in. Momentum is not a myth. It is the most powerful force in the sport, and the team that has it will usually beat the team that does not.
