# Nuclear power is the only serious answer to the climate crisis

We have spent thirty years telling ourselves a comfortable story about energy. In the story, the wind always blows, the sun always shines, and the hard choices can be left for another decade. I no longer believe the story, and I do not think the people who tell it believe it either.

Start with the promise we made. The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, commits countries to holding warming well below three degrees Celsius. Almost every government that signed it is behind on its own targets. The gap between what we promised and what we are doing is not a gap in ambition. It is a gap in power stations.

There is one technology that has already done what we need. It runs day and night, it uses very little land, and it has been cutting emissions since before most climate campaigners were born. We know it works because a country next door did it. France gets roughly two thirds of its electricity from nuclear reactors, and its power has been among the cleanest in Europe for decades.

The usual objection is fear. People remember the explosion at Chernobyl in 1986 and the meltdowns at Fukushima in 2001, and then they stop thinking. They do not ask how many people are harmed every year by the fuels that nuclear would replace. Wind and solar is welcome, and I would build more of both, but they need something steady behind them, and at present that something is usually gas.

Fear has consequences. Germany switched off its last three reactors in April 2013, a decision that look worse every winter. The reactors were paid for, they were running safely, and they were closed for reasons that had more to do with party politics than with physics.

The economics are better than the critics admit. Every euro spent on nuclear cuts three times as much carbon as a euro spent on wind. The public understands this better than the politicians do. Polls show that 70% of people who live near a reactor want a second one built.

Nobody is asking for an industry without rules. If men were angels, no government would be necessary. Reactors should be inspected hard and often, by regulators who are paid to be suspicious. But a regulator's job is to make a thing safe, not to make it impossible, and in too many countries we have confused the two.

The last objection is time. We are told that reactors take too long, and that the climate cannot wait. France built most of its reactors in about fifteen years, so any country could do the same today. All it takes is a government that decides to do it and then keep its nerve.

We could of started twenty years ago. We did not, and the second best time is now. Every year that we delay, the choice gets more harder and the bill gets bigger.
