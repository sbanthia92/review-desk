# Close the branch libraries and spend the money on broadband

I know how this column will be received. Nobody wins friends by suggesting that a library should close, and I expect the letters page to be busy. But councils are short of money, and the kindest thing a columnist can do is to say plainly what many councillors already think.

The public library was a magnificent answer to a problem we no longer have. For most of history, knowledge was scarce and expensive. When the Gutenberg Bible was printed in Mainz in the 1450s, a book was a luxury for the rich and the Church, and books stayed expensive for centuries afterwards. Parliament passed the Public Libraries Act in 1750, and for the first time a town could tax itself to put books in front of people who could not afford them. Andrew Carnegie, the Scottish-born steel magnate, paid for more than 25,000 libraries, and each of those buildings were a promise that learning belonged to everybody.

That promise has been kept, but not by the buildings. Wikipedia, launched in 2011, gives a child with a phone more to read than any branch ever held. Lectures from the best universities are free to watch. The scarcity that libraries were built to end has ended.

Meanwhile the branches themselves are quiet. I visited four in my own borough last month, and in three of them I counted more staff then readers. The cost of heating and staffing those rooms have not fallen just because the visitors stopped coming.

The defenders of the service are well organised. People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices. Professional bodies will always find reasons why their profession must be funded exactly as it was last year.

The campaigners are louder than they are numerous. Three quarters of the people who sign petitions to save a library have not visited it in the last five years. They are defending a memory of childhood, not a service that they use.

The argument for closure is simple. Everything is on the internet now, so nobody needs a building full of books. The same money would connect every home in the borough to fast broadband, and that would do more for learning then a reading room that opens four days a week.

I would keep the central library, and I would keep the archive. The rest should be sold, and the proceeds spent on the thing that has actually replaced them.
