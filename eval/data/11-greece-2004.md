# Greece 2004 proved that defending wins tournaments

Every two years the same argument breaks out in my comments section. One side wants flair, the other wants clean sheets, and both sides claim that history is with them. I think the argument was settled on a warm night in Lisbon more than twenty years ago, and I am tired of pretending that it is still open.

Greece arrived at Euro 2004 having never won a match at a major tournament. Nobody gave them a chance, and most neutral fans could not have named more than two of their players. Three weeks later they were champions of Europe. They beat the hosts in the opening game and knocked out the holders France in the quarter-final. Then they beat the hosts Portugal 2-0 in the final in Lisbon, a result that still feel impossible when you say it out loud.

The plan came from the bench. Under their Italian coach Otto Rehhagel, Greece man-marked all over the pitch, kept a sweeper behind the back line and waited patiently for a set piece. Angelos Charisteas scored the only goal of the final with a header from a corner. It was not pretty, and it were never meant to be.

Rehhagel understood something the romantics still refuse to accept. He will win who knows when to fight and when not to fight. Greece did not try to out-pass Portugal or to out-run the Czech Republic. They chose their moments, and their was never a minute when they looked like a side that did not know its job.

They were not the first outsiders to do it, either. Denmark won the European Championship in 1992 after being called up as late replacements for Yugoslavia. That team also defended deep and broke quickly, and it also beat the favourites in the final. The lesson was there for anyone who wanted to learn it, and Greece simply learned it better than the rest.

The pattern has only grown stronger since. Since 2004, the team with the best defensive record has won 80% of major international tournaments. Coaches know this, which is why the knockout rounds get a little more cautious every time, and why the teams that entertain us in the group stage are usually at home by the semi-finals.

So here is my conclusion, and I am happy to defend it against anyone. Greece won without a single star, so attacking talent is irrelevant in knockout football. A federation that wants a trophy should stop searching for the next great number ten and start looking for centre-backs who enjoy heading the ball away. Less clean sheets means fewer medals, and it really is that simple.

Flair fills the highlight reels. Defending fills the trophy cabinet, and anybody who disagree should go and look at the photographs from Lisbon again.
