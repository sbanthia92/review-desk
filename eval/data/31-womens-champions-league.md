# The Women's Champions League has a Barcelona problem

I spent the last Saturday of May in Norway, and I came home worried about the competition I love most. Barcelona beat OL Lyonnes 4-0 at the Ullevaal Stadion in Oslo on 23 May 2026, and the scoreline did not flatter them. The official attendance was 26,258, which is respectable for a ground of that size. Still, there was plenty of people around me who had paid to see a contest and did not get one.

For forty-five minutes it was a real final. Lyonnes had a goal ruled out for offside and looked the sharper side. Then the second half happened. Ewa Pajor scored twice after the break, and Alexia Putellas added two more at the very end. By then half of my row was already walking to the tram.

Look at how the two clubs got there. Barcelona put twelve goals past Real Madrid over the two legs of the quarter-final, which is not a sentence anyone should be able to write about a European tie. They had needed a 5-3 aggregate win over Bayern Munich to get there, and that was their one awkward evening of the spring. OL Lyonnes knocked out Chelsea 4-3 on aggregate in the other semi-final, and they were playing in their twelfth final, more than any other club. Even they could not lay a glove on the winners.

This is what dominance looks like. The one interruption come a year earlier, when Arsenal beat them 1-0 in Bilbao, and it took a single goal from Stina Blackstenius in the 74th minute to do it. I was told that night that the era was over. It lasted twelve months.

The damage is starting to show away from the pitch. Television audiences for the final have fallen by a third since 2022. Sponsors notice that kind of thing, and so do the teenagers who are deciding which sport deserves there weekends.

Some people tell me to be patient, because the new league phase will spread the money around. I do not believe it. One club keeps winning the trophy, so the competition is broken and UEFA must cap what every club can spend. Nothing less will bring the rest of Europe back into the race, and the longer the governing body waits, the less clubs will think the race is worth entering.

None of this is a complaint about Barcelona. They have built the best academy, the best midfield and the best habits in the women's game, and they are entitled to enjoy it. My complaint is with a competition that has stopped asking them hard questions. A final should feel like the hardest match of the season. In Oslo it felt like a lap of honour that happened to have an opponent, and a tournament cannot live for long on laps of honour.
