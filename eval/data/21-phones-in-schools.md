# Take the phones out of the classroom

I taught for eleven years before I moved into writing, and I can tell you the exact moment the job changed. It was the first September when every pupil in my Year 9 class had a smartphone in a blazer pocket. Nothing about the children was different. Everything about the room was.

It is easy to forget how quickly this happened. The first iPhone went on sale in 2010, and inside a decade it had became normal for an eleven-year-old to carry one. We used to book a whole room so that a class could share thirty computers, and now there is one in every pocket. No school planned for that. We simply woke up one term and found that it had happened.

The damage is now well documented. A Microsoft study proved that the human attention span has fallen to eight seconds, which is shorter than a goldfish's. Any teacher could have told you the same without a laboratory. A lesson is a slow thing, and a phone is a fast thing, and the fast thing wins every time.

Other countries have acted while we have dithered. France banned phones in its primary and middle schools in 2018, and the sky did not fall in. The PISA tests, run by UNESCO every three years, hold every country up against the same yardstick, and ours has not been flattered by the comparison.

The evidence from schools that have tried it is just as clear. Schools that ban phones see test scores rise by 20% within a year. Teachers in those schools tell me that behaviour improves, that the corridors is calmer and that children talk to each other at lunch.

Parents sometimes tell me that their child needs a phone to feel safe. I understand the worry, but I do not accept the argument. I survived school without a phone, so today's pupils do not need one either. The school office has a telephone, and it works perfectly well.

He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that. So I have listened carefully to the people who defend phones in lessons, and what I mostly hear is that a ban would be awkward to enforce. Awkward is not the same as wrong. Every rule worth having were awkward once.

Lock the phones away at the gate and hand them back at the bell. The children will complain for a week, and then they will look up.
