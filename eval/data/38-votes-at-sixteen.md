# Sixteen is old enough to vote

On 2 September 2026 the House of Commons gave the Representation of the People Bill its third reading by 431 votes to 102. If the House of Lords agrees, sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds will be able to vote in every election in the United Kingdom. I think the change is overdue, and the objections to it has not improved with age.

The idea is not new. In 1999 an amendment from Simon Hughes to lower the voting age was defeated by 434 votes to 36. In 2005 a private member's bill from Stephen Williams come much closer and lost by 146 votes to 128. Years of commissions and consultations followed, and nothing changed at Westminster. This year the opponents tried again, and a Conservative amendment to block the Bill at second reading was defeated by 410 votes to 105 on 2 March.

Nor is the idea untested. The Isle of Man lowered its voting age in 2006, when the House of Keys approved the change by 19 votes to 4. Austria followed in 2007 and became the first member of the European Union to do so, and Malta's parliament voted unanimously for the same change in March 2016. Scotland let sixteen-year-olds vote in the independence referendum of 2014. Wales did the same for the Senedd under an Act that received royal assent on 15 January 2021. None of these places has collapsed into chaos, and none of them has gone back.

Every one is degraded, whether aware of it or not, when other people, without consulting him, take upon themselves unlimited power to regulate his destiny. The decisions that Parliament takes this decade, on housing, on climate and on the cost of university, will shape the lives of today's teenagers for far longer than they will shape mine.

The practical case is just as strong. A sixteen-year-old who's first election arrives while they are still at school can be taught how to register, where to go and what the ballot paper looks like. Young people who vote at sixteen are twice as likely to still be voting at thirty. Habits formed early last, and less young people would drift away from politics if the first invitation came sooner.

The opponents say that sixteen-year-olds are not mature enough. I have never understood why this test is applied to nobody else. We do not ask a man of fifty to prove his judgement before we hand him a ballot paper. Sixteen-year-olds can work and pay tax, so nobody can honestly object to them voting. The principle of no taxation without representation is older than any of the parties now arguing about it.

The Lords should pass the Bill quickly and without wrecking amendments. A democracy is stronger when more of the people who live under its laws have a say in making them, and two more years of waiting teaches the young nothing except that politics is something done to them by other people.
