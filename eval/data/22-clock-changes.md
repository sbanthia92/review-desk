# Stop changing the clocks

Twice a year we all agree to be confused for a week. We turn up early, we turn up late, and we argue about whether the clock on the oven is right. I have never met anyone who enjoy it, and I have never heard a good reason to keep doing it.

The idea is younger than most people think. William Willett, a British builder, campaigned for it in a 1907 pamphlet called "The Waste of Daylight", because he was annoyed that his neighbours slept through bright summer mornings. Germany was the first country to adopt daylight saving time, in 1916, to save coal during the Second World War. Britain followed within weeks, and we have been stuck with the habit ever since.

Nobody would invent this today. The European Parliament voted in 2009 to scrap the twice-yearly change, and yet the clocks still goes back every autumn, because national governments could not agree on what should replace it.

The costs are real, even if they are spread thinly. The clock change costs the British economy two billion pounds every year. The real price of everything, what everything really costs to the man who wants to acquire it, is the toil and trouble of acquiring it. We pay for our lighter evenings in missed trains, tired children and a week of bad temper in every office in the land.

The case for abolition is short. The clocks were first changed to save coal in wartime, and we no longer light our homes with coal, so there is no reason left to change them. A rule that has outlived its purpose should be retired, however fond of it some people has become.

I do not much mind which time we choose. Lighter mornings would suit the farmers, and lighter evenings would suit the rest of us. What I mind is the switching. Pick one, write it into law, and let us never have to ask witch way the hands are meant to go again.
