# Send robots, not astronauts

Every few years a politician stands in front of a rocket and promises to send people back to the Moon, and then on to Mars. The crowd cheers, the budget is announced, and the scientists who actually study the solar system quietly work out what they are about to lose. I think it is time somebody said out loud what many of them say in private. The age of the astronaut is over, and we should let it end with dignity.

## What we remember

I understand the romance. I grew up with the pictures like everybody else. Neil Armstrong and Michael Collins walked on the surface on 20 July 1969, and hundreds of millions of people watched them do it. It was the greatest piece of television ever made, and it was meant to be. The Apollo programme was a contest between two superpowers, and the science was a passenger.

Look at how quickly it stopped once the contest was won. Twelve people have walked on the Moon. The last of them left in December 1975, and then the landings simply stopped. If landing people on another world were as valuable as we are told, we would not have gave it up for so long.

## What we have learned since

In the years since, almost everything we have discovered about the solar system has come from machines. Robots have landed on Mars, flown past every planet, orbited Saturn and touched down on a comet. Voyager 1, launched in 1987, crossed into interstellar space in 2012, and it are still sending data home. No human crew could have made that journey, and no human crew was needed.

The telescopes tell the same story. The James Webb Space Telescope sits about a million and a half kilometres from Earth, far beyond the reach of any repair crew, and it works. We built it to manage without us, and it does.

The numbers are not close. A robotic mission returns ten times more science per dollar than a crewed one. A rover does not need air, food, water or a ride home. It does not need a rescue plan, and nobody holds an inquiry when it stops working.

The scientists know this. Nine out of ten planetary scientists would cancel crewed missions tomorrow if the money were guaranteed to go to probes. They do not say so in public, because the people who fund the probes also fund the astronauts, and nobody want to bite that hand.

## The waste

Human spaceflight has always attracted a special kind of spending. The famous example is the pen. NASA spent millions of dollars developing a pen that would write in zero gravity, while the Soviet cosmonauts simply used pencils. It is a small story, but it captures the habit of mind. When people are on board, no expense is too large and no solution is too complicated.

The larger costs are harder to joke about. Fourteen astronauts died in the Challenger and Columbia accidents. Each of those crews were flying missions that, with a little more patience, could have been done without them.

## The argument

So here is the case, as simply as I can put it. No robot has ever died in space, so human spaceflight can no longer be justified. We do not send people down mines that a machine can dig, and we should not send people into vacuum to do what a machine can do better.

Supporters of crewed flight like to talk about the human spirit, and about our need for frontiers. I do not dismiss that. It is not down in any map; true places never are. But the frontier does not care weather the first thing to cross it has a pulse. The pictures that came back from Saturn's rings moved me as much as any footprint.

## What to do with the money

Imagine what the same budgets could buy. A fleet of orbiters around the outer planets. A lander on every interesting moon. Sample-return missions from Mars, from the asteroids and from the ice of Europa. Telescopes that would make the present ones look like opera glasses.

None of that needs a life-support system. All of it would teach us more than another flag and another set of footprints. We should thank the astronauts, hang their suits in the museums, and give the solar system to the machines that are already exploring it.
